On Justice and Credit: Microlending Companies are not Serving the People

Read the full paper ↗ See the interactive model ↗

In a research paper for Stanford’s writing program (PWR), I looked at how for-profit microlending in Latin America drifted away from its original purpose. Microcredit began as a development tool, but many lenders now charge sky-high interest rates that deepen borrower vulnerability instead of promoting long-term development.

Microlending is still a sound idea: the evidence shows small positive or neutral effects for borrowers, and the finance gap for small businesses in developing countries is about US$5.7 trillion. The problem is how it is being done.

My proposal is a regulatory cap on microlending interest rates. Earlier caps in Latin America failed because lenders could not tell good borrowers from risky ones. Machine learning credit scoring is already in use, but a cap would give lenders the incentive to push it much further and compete on better risk models instead of higher rates, while people who lose access move to low-interest government programs.

Extended

Borrow, pay. Borrow, pay.

Pina, a 58-year-old baker in Oaxaca, lost her job during the pandemic while saving to pay for a piece of land. No traditional bank would lend to her, so she took a $500 loan from Banco Compartamos at a 100% interest rate. She could not repay on time, so she borrowed again, and again, until she had six loans at sky-high rates from different lenders. In her words: “Before you know it, I am borrowing to pay.”

Her story is not rare. 55.2% of Mexico’s workforce works in informal and micro-enterprises, which produced 25.4% of the country’s GDP in 2024. Millions of people like Pina have fragile finances and no access to normal credit.

Why microlending still makes sense

Microloans are small, usually $700 to $1,500, and go to low-income people and entrepreneurs who cannot get traditional credit. Done well, they could help women gain financial independence and make small businesses more resilient. In Latin America, only 35% of women had a formal bank account in 2014, against 44% of men.

The evidence is modest but not negative. A study of randomized evaluations in six countries found microcredit is not transformative, but it gives people more freedom in their choices. A study of the Brazilian fintech Avante found access to credit raised monthly revenues and profits by about 4.5%. And demand is huge: supply of credit grew about 7% a year between 2015 and 2019, yet the finance gap kept growing by more than 6% a year.

From Grameen Bank to Wall Street

Microlending was formally born in 1983 with Grameen Bank in Bangladesh, created by Dr. Muhammad Yunus, who won the Nobel Peace Prize in 2006. Grameen is owned by its borrowers and charges very low interest rates, so its slim profits go back to the poor.

Banco Compartamos started with a similar mission in 1990, but it went private in 2001 to raise more money. By 2007 it had $81.5 million in revenue, and after an IPO valued at $1.53 billion, its executives were rewarded with $150 million.

Why profit and mission rarely mix

A values-led microlender sits between a charity and a business. To protect borrowers, its rates must stay low, but low rates make it hard to earn the returns investors expect. Donors get tax breaks and recognition, and investors get profits, so a company that offers neither struggles to raise money.

Tala shows the difficulty: it reached an $800 million valuation but, after 12 years, still is not profitable. My takeaway: if you see a high-yield microlending company, be wary of its impact on the people it says it serves.

Sky-high interest rates

Lenders have real reasons for higher rates: high operating costs, defaults, investors’ expected returns and inflation. But the numbers go far beyond that. This is the total annual cost of a loan, as published on Banco Compartamos’ own website:

The rates are stated openly, but many low-income borrowers lack the financial literacy to judge what they mean. It is not only microlenders: Nubank was reported charging some Brazilian customers a 455% annual cost on credit cards. As one finance professor put it, the system treats “every borrower as if they were the worst possible borrower.”

“For the people”

Banco Compartamos, Nubank and Tala all describe their missions as financial inclusion and empowerment, while their business is built on profitability. Not every high-rate loan is harmful, but there is a clear trend: to compete, lenders borrow the philanthropic story even when their impact does not match it.

My proposal: cap the rates

Lowering rates sounds like the obvious fix, but a cap has side effects. Profits drop, so lenders stop lending to riskier clients and ask for more data, leaving many people without credit. Ecuador and Nicaragua tried caps around 2007, and they did not work, not because the idea was bad, but because the technology was not there yet.

Today it is. Machine learning credit scoring is already being used: lenders build risk models on alternative data such as social media activity, shopping patterns or location, instead of relying only on a FICO score. But while high rates stay legal, charging more is easier than scoring better. With a cap in place, lenders would have the incentive to develop far more innovative risk models and profit from them, not from higher rates. People who lose access for a while could move to government programs with zero or very low interest, which would cost governments less because they would serve a smaller group.
